The First Trophy

Do managers win early, or are they just not given the time?

Most managers who win something do it in their first season. That sounds like a law of football: win early, or not at all. But there is another explanation. Maybe managers only win early because most of them never get a second season.

This article follows all 1,187 reigns in the dataset, from the season a manager took over to the season he left, and looks at when the first trophy came. A reign is a run of consecutive seasons as manager of the season at one club.

The headline: win early

Of the 1,152 reigns that have ended, 398 brought at least one trophy: just over a third. And of those 398, 284 delivered their first trophy in the very first season. Late bloomers are rare. Among long reigns that won something, only a handful had to wait until the fourth season or later.

Read like that, the lesson for a club seems simple: if it has not worked after a season or two, it will not work. But that conclusion skips a step.

Chicken or egg

Most managers never get the chance to win late. Of the 754 reigns that ended without a trophy, 486 were over after a single season and another 170 after two. A first season is the only season most managers ever get, so of course that is where most trophies are won.

The fair question is therefore a different one: of the managers who did get another season, how many won something in it? Seen that way, the curve is almost flat.

Data Focus: The Chance of a First Trophy, Season by Season

Of all managers still in charge and still without a trophy at the start of season n, the share who won their first trophy in that season.

  1. Season 1 25% (296 of 1,187)
  2. Season 2 20% (79 of 391)
  3. Season 3 19% (26 of 137)
  4. Season 4 22% (10 of 46)
  5. Season 5 and later 3 of 24

A manager who survives a trophyless season has roughly the same chance of winning in the next one as a newcomer has in his first. The chance does not collapse after year one; the patience does. "Win early or not at all" is mostly a description of how clubs behave, not of how football works.

One caution. The managers who are given a second or third season without a trophy are not a random group: their clubs saw something worth keeping. The flat curve shows that time is not wasted on them, but not that every manager would have won with more time.

The starting point matters more

What does make a big difference is the club a manager inherits. Split the reigns by whether the club had won anything in the three seasons before the appointment, and two very different jobs appear.

Same measure as above: the share of managers still without a trophy who won one in that season.

A manager who takes over a winning club is three times as likely to win in his first season as one who takes over a club that has stopped winning. At a winning club the chance is highest straight away and fades the longer the wait lasts. At a club that has not won for a while, it starts low and rises slightly with time. Patience pays off least where trophies are already expected, and most where a club has to be rebuilt.

The ones who waited

The managers who won their first trophy in their fourth season or later are few, but the list is remarkable. Hennes Weisweiler waited until his sixth season at Borussia Mönchengladbach for the 1970 league title, and then turned the club into one of the best in Europe. Bill Shankly needed five seasons at Liverpool before his first league title in 1964. Alex Ferguson won his first trophy, the FA Cup of 1990, in his fourth season. Jürgen Klopp won the Champions League in his fourth season at Liverpool, counting the half season in which he arrived. Ottmar Hitzfeld at Borussia Dortmund, Bertie Mee at Arsenal and Louis van Gaal at AZ all waited four seasons too.

Thirteen reigns in the dataset brought a first trophy in the fourth season or later. Ten of them started at a club that had won nothing in the three seasons before; Ferguson, who inherited the FA Cup winners of 1985, is one of the exceptions. And most of these managers went on to stay for a long time: a late first trophy is often the start of a dynasty rather than the end of a wait.

What this means

The data does not say that managers either win at once or never. It says that most clubs do not wait to find out. Managers who are given time keep winning at about the same rate, and for managers who take over a club that has stopped winning, the chance even grows. The clubs that let them get on with it, Mönchengladbach with Weisweiler, Liverpool with Shankly and Klopp, Manchester United with Ferguson, were rewarded for it.

How this was measured: a reign is a run of consecutive seasons as manager of the season at one club; the first season is the one in which he became manager of the season, even if he arrived during it. Trophies are league titles, national cups and major European trophies, with at most one of each per season, credited to the manager of the season. Reigns still running in 2025/26 count in the season-by-season figures but not in the totals of reigns that have ended. Data up to and including 2025/26.
